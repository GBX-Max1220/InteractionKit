# Study 2 Source Shortlist — Batch 02 (Remaining Strong Candidates)

**Material version:** `study2-candidates-v0.1`
**Status:** metadata and abstract-level relevance checked; independent domain review pending
**Access date:** 2026-08-02

This batch covers the 11 strong-consensus candidates not included in Batch 01. “Advance” means that two sources plausibly match the current decision target; it is not a final evidence or ground-truth judgment.

## Advance to full dossier

### `strong_03` — repeated sleep restriction

1. Craven J, et al. “Effects of Acute Sleep Loss on Physical Performance: A Systematic and Meta-Analytical Review.” *Sports Medicine*. 2022. DOI: `10.1007/s40279-022-01706-y`; PMID: `35708888`.
2. Charest J, et al. “Effects of Acute Sleep Deprivation on Sporting Performance in Athletes: A Comprehensive Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.” *Nature and Science of Sleep*. 2024. DOI: `10.2147/NSS.S467531`; PMID: `39006249`.

Decision: advance. Both syntheses support performance costs, but the scenario says repeated restriction whereas the strongest retrieved syntheses emphasize acute loss. The full dossier must add a chronic/repeated-restriction source or narrow the wording.

### `strong_07` — suspected exertional heat illness

1. Roberts WO, et al. “ACSM Expert Consensus Statement on Exertional Heat Illness: Recognition, Management, and Return to Activity.” *Current Sports Medicine Reports*. 2023. DOI: `10.1249/JSR.0000000000001058`; PMID: `37036463`.
2. Belval LN, et al. “Consensus Statement—Prehospital Care of Exertional Heat Stroke.” *Prehospital Emergency Care*. 2018. DOI: `10.1080/10903127.2017.1392666`; PMID: `29336710`.

Decision: advance with wording review. The sources strongly support stopping activity and rapid cooling for suspected exertional heat stroke. The current phrase “symptoms compatible with heat illness” may be broader than heat stroke and must be adjudicated so that the foil is not unsafe for a minor heat condition.

### `strong_08` — managed heat acclimation

1. Racinais S, et al. “Consensus recommendations on training and competing in the heat.” *British Journal of Sports Medicine*. 2015. DOI: `10.1136/bjsports-2015-094915`; PMID: `26069301`.
2. Brown HA, et al. “Quantifying Exercise Heat Acclimatisation in Athletes and Military Personnel: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis.” *Sports Medicine*. 2024. DOI: `10.1007/s40279-023-01972-4`; PMID: `38051495`.

Decision: advance. Both sources address repeated heat exposure and physiological strain. The dossier must preserve protocol and sex/population limitations rather than claim one universal exposure schedule.

### `strong_09` — aerobic training for an inactive adult

1. Bull FC, et al. “World Health Organization 2020 guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behaviour.” *British Journal of Sports Medicine*. 2020. DOI: `10.1136/bjsports-2020-102955`; PMID: `33239350`.
2. Huang T, et al. “Effects of exercise on body fat percentage and cardiorespiratory fitness in sedentary adults: a systematic review and network meta-analysis.” *Frontiers in Public Health*. 2025. DOI: `10.3389/fpubh.2025.1624562`; PMID: `40746688`.

Decision: advance. The sources support exercise for inactive adults, but the network meta-analysis does not make aerobic training uniquely superior for every cardiorespiratory outcome. Retain only the contrast with flexibility as the main fitness stimulus, not a broad modality-superiority claim.

### `strong_11` — load progression after loss of control

1. American College of Sports Medicine. “American College of Sports Medicine Position Stand. Resistance Training Prescription for Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults: An Overview of Reviews.” *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*. 2026. DOI: `10.1249/MSS.0000000000003897`; PMID: `41843416`.
2. American College of Sports Medicine. “Progression models in resistance training for healthy adults.” *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*. 2009. DOI: `10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181915670`; PMID: `19204579`.

Decision: advance with a source gap. The position stands support progressive, individualized loading, but neither source alone may establish “controlled technique before load increase” as a binary rule across complex exercises. A technique-specific consensus or a narrower movement context is required.

### `strong_12` — sustained energy deficit

1. Jensen MD, et al. “2013 AHA/ACC/TOS guideline for the management of overweight and obesity in adults.” *Circulation*. 2014. DOI: `10.1161/01.cir.0000437739.71477.ee`; PMID: `24222017`.
2. Wu X, et al. “Comparison of Different Intermittent Fasting Patterns or Different Extents of Calorie Restriction for Weight Loss and Metabolic Improvement in Adults: A Systematic Review and Network Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials.” *Nutrition Reviews*. 2026. DOI: `10.1093/nutrit/nuaf056`; PMID: `40367516`.

Decision: advance. The newer synthesis reports that weight-loss effectiveness primarily tracks energy-restriction magnitude rather than meal-timing pattern. The dossier must limit the population and avoid prescribing an exact deficit.

### `strong_14` — resistance training for older adults

1. Radaelli R, et al. “Effects of Resistance Training Volume on Physical Function, Lean Body Mass and Lower-Body Muscle Hypertrophy and Strength in Older Adults: A Systematic Review and Network Meta-analysis of 151 Randomised Trials.” *Sports Medicine*. 2025. DOI: `10.1007/s40279-024-02123-z`; PMID: `39405023`.
2. Lai CC, et al. “Effects of resistance training, endurance training and whole-body vibration on lean body mass, muscle strength and physical performance in older people: a systematic review and network meta-analysis.” *Age and Ageing*. 2018. DOI: `10.1093/ageing/afy009`; PMID: `29471456`.

Decision: advance. Both syntheses support resistance training for strength/function in older adults. The current comparison against an aerobic-and-mobility-only program should be framed as inclusion of resistance training, not rejection of aerobic training.

### `strong_15` — symptoms and reduced tolerance at altitude

1. Luks AM, et al. “Wilderness Medical Society Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Prevention, Diagnosis, and Treatment of Acute Altitude Illness: 2024 Update.” *Wilderness & Environmental Medicine*. 2024. DOI: `10.1016/j.wem.2023.05.013`; PMID: `37833187`.
2. Bergeron MF, et al. “International Olympic Committee consensus statement on thermoregulatory and altitude challenges for high-level athletes.” *British Journal of Sports Medicine*. 2012. DOI: `10.1136/bjsports-2012-091296`; PMID: `22685119`.

Decision: advance with population caution. The WMS guideline addresses illness; the IOC statement addresses athletes. Reviewers must decide whether “substantial symptoms and reduced tolerance” is specific enough to distinguish ordinary performance decrement from altitude illness.

## Hold before further sourcing

### `strong_06` — new chest pain during exercise

Candidate sources: the 2021 AHA/ACC chest-pain guideline and the American College of Cardiology athlete chest-pain review.

Decision: hold. “Chest pain” has cardiac, pulmonary, gastrointestinal, and musculoskeletal causes, and the source search did not yield two scenario-matched exercise guidelines supporting the exact binary action. Revise toward defined exertional pressure/tightness with red flags, or reject to avoid converting generic medical caution into a research ground truth.

### `strong_10` — severe worsening symptoms after hard exercise

Candidate source class: exertional-rhabdomyolysis clinical reviews and emergency guidance.

Decision: hold. The prompt does not identify the symptoms that distinguish ordinary delayed-onset muscle soreness from possible rhabdomyolysis or another acute condition. Add specific red flags such as marked weakness, swelling, or dark urine before retrieving condition-specific sources; otherwise reject.

### `strong_16` — acute traumatic injury with loss of function

Candidate source class: injury-specific clinical decision rules and acute musculoskeletal guidelines.

Decision: hold. “Acute traumatic musculoskeletal injury” spans fracture, dislocation, tendon rupture, sprain, and contusion. No single evidence dossier can validate one binary rule across that space. Replace with a named injury/context or reject.
