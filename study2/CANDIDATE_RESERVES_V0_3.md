## `mixed_08` — pre-exercise feeding for prolonged aerobic performance

Replaces the fasted-versus-fed fat-loss item. The prior outcome was long-term fat loss, but its best available source evidence concerned acute substrate oxidation; that mismatch did not support a unique answer.

New decision context: a healthy endurance athlete can tolerate food before a prolonged morning aerobic performance session.

Provisional preferred decision: eat an appropriate pre-exercise meal rather than begin after an overnight fast.

Source path:

1. Aird TP, Davies RW, Carson BP. “Effects of fasted vs fed-state exercise on performance and post-exercise metabolism: A systematic review and meta-analysis.” *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports*. 2018;28(5):1476–1493. DOI: `10.1111/sms.13054`; PMID: `29315892`. The meta-analysis found that pre-exercise feeding enhanced prolonged, but not shorter-duration, aerobic performance.
2. Thomas DT, Erdman KA, Burke LM. “American College of Sports Medicine Joint Position Statement. Nutrition and Athletic Performance.” *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*. 2016;48(3):543–568. DOI: `10.1249/MSS.0000000000000852`; PMID: `26891166`. The joint position statement supports matching food and nutrient timing to performance demands.

Why it is conditionally scorable: the prompt fixes the target outcome as prolonged-session performance and states that food is tolerated. Fasted training may still be selected for other metabolic-adaptation goals, and meal size, timing, gastrointestinal response, and carbohydrate availability remain boundaries.

## `mixed_13` — autoregulated loading for variable readiness

Replaces the two-versus-four weekly hypertrophy-frequency item. When weekly volume is equated, the original alternatives were both defensible and did not provide a unique ground truth.

New decision context: a resistance-trained athlete has a strength-focused program and meaningful day-to-day readiness variation.

Provisional preferred decision: adjust working loads with predefined performance-feedback rules rather than use scheduled percentage loads without a session-level adjustment.

Source path:

1. Zhang M, et al. “Comparison of Velocity and Percentage-based Training on Maximal Strength: Meta-analysis.” *International Journal of Sports Medicine*. 2022;43(12):981–995. DOI: `10.1055/a-1790-8546`; PMID: `35255509`. The synthesis reported a small overall advantage for velocity-based prescription and identified athlete population as an important boundary.
2. Zhang X, et al. “Auto-Regulation Method vs. Fixed-Loading Method in Maximum Strength Training for Athletes: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.” *Frontiers in Physiology*. 2021;12:651112. DOI: `10.3389/fphys.2021.651112`; PMID: `33776802`; PMCID: `PMC7994759`. The meta-analysis favored autoregulated methods over fixed loading for maximal-strength outcomes in trained athletes.

Why it is conditionally scorable: the population, strength outcome, and variable-readiness context support autoregulation, while the advantage depends on the feedback method, measurement quality, exercise, training status, and program duration. The item does not claim that one autoregulation method or threshold is universally superior.
